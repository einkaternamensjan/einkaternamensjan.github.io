# Love and Lolita

The text that follows is probably the saddest I have written so far. I wanted, though, to try to discuss the possibly true aspects of love that are to be found in the book "Lolita", without any real aim beyond the discussion itself and perhaps a kind of review of the book. Although the content, that is, the relationship the book deals with, amounts to a tragic and repugnant abuse of power, it hurts one even more terribly in that it also sets out the true sufferings of love. Over the course of this text I have had to think "leaving aside…" with regard to the appalling events that happen in the course of the book. That has caused me as much unease as it still does whenever I have to read again the sentences in which this reading consists. But it is unfortunately fundamental to the reading of a book that describes such terrible events.
So let us now get to the matter with the first example. Nabokov writes:

"The words 'for ever' refer only to my own passion, to the eternal Lolita as she is to be found in my blood. The Lolita whose crista iliaca is not yet pronounced, the Lolita I could touch, smell, hear and see today, the Lolita of the shrill voice and the rich brown hair." [^1]

What shows itself here, in my view, is the failure of sense-certainty. As in Hegel, in his Phenomenology of Spirit. Spirit notices that the "Now" it points out at a given moment has already ceased to exist as soon as it has been pointed out. Hegel writes: "The Now is pointed out; this Now. Now; it has already ceased to be in being pointed out; the Now that is, is another than the one pointed out, and we see that the Now is just this: in being, already no longer to be."[^2] He even goes a little further. For the speaker, Humbert Humbert, points out *his* Lolita as a Here, in the way Hegel describes. The speaker thus overcomes this contradiction by pointing out the eternal Lolita. For he now knows that *his* Lolita is not the immediate Lolita, but a construction of his brain, one that is not commensurable with Dolores "in and for herself". Just as Adorno criticises Hegel and Heidegger for it, Humbert too gives the universal priority over the existent, over the non-identical. The eternal *Lolita* consumes the real girl, Dolores.

"Perception takes what is, for it, the existent."[^3] His Lolita is at once what is for him. He *perceives* her. But as she is in herself, and more will be said about this later, she is a different Lolita. The Lolita that is hers. The problem that shows itself here is not one that can simply be struck out. For it is one of the most significant problems of being in love. Humbert Humbert already knows about this contradiction. He loves Lolita, but knows that she is only *Lolita*, not Dolores, and loves her anyway. He does not shout himself hoarse with indignation at having been treated like a fool, no. He knows that *his* Lolita and *Dolores* diverge. So how is love possible, with a contradiction like this immanent to it? Should it not fail because of it? As soon as one notices that the beloved is not the beloved? The possible answer can be located in the fact that, just as Dolores, as she is *in herself*, does not correspond to the speaker's idea of her, so the speaker, as he is *in himself*, also differs from his own idea of himself. His love rests on a reification of the character he perceives himself to have. His idea, or his conception, of *his* Lolita rests on his idea or conception of himself. The qualities he consciously loves are mirrored in his conscious knowledge of himself. The qualities he subconsciously loves are likewise mirrored in his subconscious knowledge of himself. What he knows about himself he can therefore connect with a quality of Lolita's that, as he senses it, goes together with it. Subconsciously, however, this is hard to explain. Suppose that desire is desire for recognition, so that the unconscious, the origin of desire, strives for recognition; then it could be inferred that Humbert's subconscious knowledge of his own qualities seeks recognition in the form of Lolita's opposing qualities, for instance her childlike innocence as the victim against his monstrous guilt as the perpetrator. If instead one adopts the idea of the "desiring-machine", introduced by Deleuze and Guattari, then it could also be extrapolated that Humbert's unconscious itself produces the wish for Lolita. That his unconscious, then, does not take the world in and get influenced by it, his desires coming from outside, but that his unconscious spits the world out and desires it at the same time. His craving for Lolita has been produced by himself. He knows about himself, if not always consciously, but his idea of himself is a different one from the one that corresponds to the truth. He also knows that his idea of Dolores, that is, Lolita, does not correspond to the truth about her. So he lets the untruths converge. If one of them falls short, the other is at hand. Since Humbert Humbert writes about himself under the pseudonym "Humbert Humbert", it becomes clear that his idea of the beloved evades not just one truth, but two. This is how he manages to withstand the immanent contradiction. Because the one untruth (Lolita) seeks its corrective in the other untruth (Humbert Humbert), the whole thing shows itself here as a closed circle. Neither of the two has to reach out into the external world to fetch anything; instead they were created by the narrator for the purpose of completing one another. Within the context of the book this is entertaining enough, but if one considers what it would mean in one's own life, it becomes clear that this work presents itself as a kind of parody. Whether intended or not, there is no escaping that painful fact. The desideratum of Humbert's love is the untrue, and so is its origin. What is painful about it is, as I see it, the reflection carried out at this point in the book, namely that one's own desire consists in a deception one has produced oneself. One could suggest here that a society which favours and privileges the exchange of commodities contributes to this confusion. But I have neither the inclination nor the space for it in this text.

The passage that immediately follows this quotation also shows the narrator's fear of the passing of time. He (Nabokov) writes:

"She will be thirteen on 1 January. In two years she will stop being a nymphet. […] *That* Lolita, *My* Lolita, poor Catullus would lose for ever."[^4]

The narrator shows here that he knows perfectly well that the object of his craving will disappear. He also knows, since he has already admitted it, that *his Lolita* does not exist in reality. So he is playing with the unreal idea of an unreal object. For we see over the course of the book how Dolores cannot bear the ordeal. How she cries every night. How, after treatment in hospital, she does not let the chance slip and escapes the narrator. Although everything is tolerable inside the narrator's head, the whole thing collapses in reality. But does it fail here because of contradictions immanent to love? Caution is needed here, because what is at issue, in my view, is not love but rather a desire, a degenerate obsession on the narrator's part, one that is not conceived as reciprocal from the side of its object. The harshest criticisms of the book are aimed precisely at the fact that Dolores is given no voice of her own, that she is treated like a stowaway. But this is exactly what is essential about the story itself. Nabokov tries, that is, through the narrator's hand, to convince the reader subliminally that what is at issue is love. At the same time he withholds from Dolores every possibility of reflecting the desire back. The voice Dolores does not get is what the crime is founded on, because if she had been given a voice of her own, one would run the risk that the story could after all be presented as a degenerate kind of love. The narrator quotes Lolita contradicting him now and then, but he never lets it get out of hand. Dolores has to remain voiceless so that the depravity of the desire cannot be waved away.
Nabokov is describing here a process that any love, or any desire, can go through. The constant reification of Dolores, the reified Dolores who is called Lolita, is, leaving aside the other uncomfortable facts, a symptom of a failed love that appears all too often in reality. The digitalisation of the dating sphere has the consequence that potential partners are left, for longer stretches, with no protection against their own idealisation. Although this was always the case, since one could idealise a person just as well before the age of dating apps, the process during which one is made to appear as a mere ideal is prolonged. For the process of any dating app rests on fantasy. One can, admittedly, talk to a potential match, but the first impression here does not consist in the conversation, but in the photos, in the banal facts about that person's interests. Idealisation therefore predominates during the process of getting to know someone, which creates the possibility of no longer being able to strike it out. Reification thus becomes the protagonist of modern love more often than the person herself. Reification, by its nature, has no voice of its own, only the one lent to it. Another case of this is the idealisation of celebrities. Take Taylor Swift, for example, who has built up a parasocial relationship with a fanbase of millions of people. Her presence on stage may well be breathtaking; my aim here is not to dispute that. But the fans who love her so much are not connected to the person, they are attached to the idea of "Taylor Swift". The Taylor Swift discussed in newspaper articles and on the evening news is not Taylor Swift, but a hypostatised reification that allows people to lend her their own voices. The celebrity "Taylor Swift" is able to make a statement now and then in response to some recent event, but because of her own socialisation every statement stays veiled by the hidden motives her fans imagine for themselves.

In both of these cases what we are dealing with is a certain absence, as with Humbert. For in both cases the bodily aspects of the people are present, that is, available, but the voice is always a distorted one, or rather a non-existent one.
In the first case it is the absence of the potential match. An absence that is covered over by the dating apps staging the constant presence of their users. According to the dating apps, they have made the process of dating easier by abolishing its temporal limits. It is now supposedly possible to find a partner at any hour. One no longer has to wait for the end of the working day, or for the weekend; every moment is now the right one to set off in search of love. The structure of the violence Humbert does to Dolores, by turning her into Lolita and robbing her of her voice, can be observed in the process of the dating app as well. This time subliminally incited by the company responsible and carried out by the users themselves. So what presents itself as the presence of an incidental match is in reality that match's absence. It seems important to me to note at this point that the violence meant here is not the rape of a child, but the conceptual structure of violence that makes such a rape possible in the first place. The structure of the violence consists in robbing the other, the reified human being, of every voice except the one lent to them by the signifier, so that such outrages can be normalised. I do not want to discuss this at any length here, but the rise of the male "supremacy propaganda" spread on social media, and the accompanying perceptions of other people, above all women in this case, certainly contribute to the rise in recorded offences in recent years. Through the rise in reported violence, however, also shows itself the possible willingness to stop letting it be swept under the carpet and to stand up to Perpatrators.[^5]

In the second case the absence is somewhat more complicated, since Swift is aware of her own pretence. Unlike in the first case, where the reification proceeds unconsciously from both sides and is staged by a third party, here it proceeds consciously from at least one side. For Swift deploys this false identity she has invented in order to maintain her status in the world of contemporary culture. Through calculated marketing tricks this false identity is spread and staged as the *real* one. The Taylor Swift of the masses is the Taylor Swift of the real Taylor Swift's imagination, that is, the reified Taylor Swift. Because she has had to reify herself in order to be able to extend her success. This reification therefore takes up the place that was, so to speak, reserved for it, but it is not laid to rest here. In order to maintain the status, the reification has to change, mutate, keep up with the times. So the reification has to make itself capable of being hypostatised. The reified Taylor Swift is hypostatised by fans in response to the real Taylor Swift's actions. Suddenly the sham Swift exists alongside the real one without this creating any problems. In this way it has been possible to expose the lie that the marketing promise does not concern the real celebrity, and at the same time to maintain the sham celebrity.[^6] Just as Humbert Humbert represents the untrue identity of the narrator and Lolita the untrue identity of Dolores, so the hypostatisation represents one untrue identity and the reification a second, equally untrue one. Here the hypostatisation can seek its corrective in the reification and vice versa. The consequence of this is that Swift's voice is split in two. She has robbed herself of her voice so that it can be attributed to one of the untrue Swifts. In this way she remains voiceless, like Lolita, so that the obsession, or the desire, for her can be maintained. As in Lolita's case, both invented identities come from the same hand. Here, though, Swift is not trying to hold her own world together, but the world of her fans. So instead of deceiving herself in order to go on believing that she is loved, she deceives the public in order to convince it that she loves it. Swift, in her case, is the opposite of what Dolores represents. She tries to hold the gap between the unreal and the real as far apart as possible, so that she can profit from it as much as possible. Whereas the rupture in Dolores's case, the crying at night, represents the failure, the rupture in Swift's case is by contrast a sign of success. Her voicelessness is exactly what grants her power, whereas in Dolores's case it testifies to her powerlessness. The same movement has a different outcome, and this is above all because Swift withholds her own voice from herself, whereas Dolores is robbed of her voice by Humbert. Swift can let her figures take the credit on her behalf, but as soon as something is misinterpreted by the masses, she can reclaim her voice at any time by suggesting that it was outside her control and that her powerlessness is being exploited. The basis of her power springs from her self-created powerlessness. A momentary failure of the Swiftian message could be traced back, and has several times been allowed to be traced back, to a distortion of that message by the industry. As soon as Dolores is given her own voice shortly before the end of the book, Humbert's world collapses. If, on the other hand, Swift plays the role of both the thief of the voice and the one robbed of it, then every collapse works out to her advantage. She can demolish the world of the media, present herself as a feminist campaigning for her rights, since they are always only hers, to exist as a woman in the music industry. But at the same time she can have herself portrayed as powerless, in a system that is always working against her. Hardly credible, considering that she is probably the most influential person in the global music industry. Every step of her power is self-confirming, so to speak. She plays Humbert and Dolores at the same time. But this theory does not apply to Taylor Swift alone. The same can be applied to all major pop stars, if not to celebrities in general. The game of victim and perpetrator has proved to be, according to my theory, the most effective perfidy in all matters of holding on to power. As Debord writes: "It is well known that the admirable people in whom the system personifies itself are not what they are; they became great men by sinking below the reality of the humblest individual life, and everyone knows it."[^7] Through this the stories of the real victims are suppressed and eliminated, diminished and trivialised, in order to make room for another superstar whose life is supposed to represent an instantiation of suffering as such: "Two souls, alas, dwell in my breast![^8]" Humbert created Lolita in order to control Dolores. Swift created Swift in order to control Swift.

I am coming, I think, to the end of my reflections. A mere attempt is probably the better name for it. The central themes of the book hide behind a curtain of disgust. It is worth reading the book, in my opinion, provided one does not give in to this disgust. For one does recoil in disgust from the content, and the sad fact is that one has to leave it aside in order to be able to go on reading the book. Whether this came about by design I do not know. Although the material is abrasive, it nevertheless testifies to Nabokov's insights.
