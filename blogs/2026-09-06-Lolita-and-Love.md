# Love and Lolita

The text that now follows is likely the saddest I have written thus far. I wished nonetheless to attempt to discuss those aspects of love, possibly true ones, that are to be found in the book "Lolita", without any proper aim beyond the discussion itself and perhaps a sort of review of the book. Although the content, that is, the relationship with which the book deals, constitutes a tragic and repugnant abuse of power, it hurts one still more dreadfully in that it also sets forth the true sufferings of love. In the course of this text I have had to think "leaving aside…" with regard to the abominable events that occur over the course of the book. This has caused me as much unease as it continues to do whenever I must read again the sentences in which this reading consists. But it is, unfortunately, fundamental to the reading of a book that describes such terrible events.

So let us now come to the matter at hand, with the first example. Nabokov writes:

"The word "forever" referred only to my own passion, to the eternal Lolita as reflected in my blood. The Lolita whose iliac crests had not yet flared, the Lolita that today I could touch and smell and hear and see, the Lolita of the strident voice and the rich brown hair." [^1]

Here, to my mind, the failure of sense-certainty shows itself. As with Hegel in his Phenomenology of Spirit. Spirit notices that the "Now" which it points out at a given moment has already ceased to exist as soon as it has been pointed out. Thus Hegel writes: "The Now is pointed out; this Now. Now; it has already ceased to be in the very act of being pointed out; the Now that is, is an other than the one pointed out, and we see that the Now is just this: in being, already no longer to be."[^2] He goes even a little further. For the speaker, Humbert Humbert, points out *his* Lolita as a Here, as Hegel describes it. Thus the speaker overcomes this contradiction by pointing out the eternal Lolita. For he knows henceforth that *his* Lolita is not the immediate Lolita, but a construction of his brain, one that is not commensurable with Dolores "in and for herself". As Adorno reproaches Hegel and Heidegger for doing, Humbert too grants the universal precedence over the existent, over the non-identical. The eternal *Lolita* consumes the real girl, Dolores.

"Perception takes that which is, for it, the existent."[^3] His Lolita is at once that which, for him, is. He *perceives* her. But she is, as she is in herself, of which more will be said later, another Lolita. The Lolita that is hers. The problem that here makes itself known is not one that can simply be struck out. For it is one of the most significant problems of being in love. Humbert Humbert already knows of this contradiction. He loves Lolita, knows meanwhile that she is merely *Lolita*, not Dolores, and loves her all the same. He does not shout himself hoarse in indignation at having been treated like a fool, no. He knows that *his* Lolita and *Dolores* diverge from one another. How, then, is love possible, with such a contradiction immanent to it? Should it not founder upon this? As soon as one notices that the beloved is not the beloved? The possible answer may be located in the fact that, just as Dolores, as she is *in herself*, does not correspond to the speaker's conception of her, so the speaker, as he is *in himself*, likewise differs from his own conception of himself. His love rests upon a reification of the character he perceives himself to have. His conception, or his apprehension, of *his* Lolita rests upon his conception or apprehension of himself. The qualities he consciously loves are mirrored in his conscious knowledge of himself. The qualities he subconsciously loves are likewise mirrored in his subconscious knowledge of himself. What he knows about himself he can therefore connect with a quality of Lolita's that, by his intimation, goes together with it. Subconsciously, however, this can scarcely be explained. Let us assume that desire is desire for recognition, such that the unconscious, the origin of desire, strives after recognition; then it might be inferred that Humbert's subconscious knowledge of his own qualities seeks recognition in the form of Lolita's opposing qualities — for instance, her childlike innocence as victim set against his monstrous guilt as perpetrator. If, on the other hand, one adopts the idea of the "desiring-machine", introduced by Deleuze and Guattari, then it might equally be extrapolated that Humbert's unconscious itself produces the wish for Lolita. That his unconscious, then, does not take in the world and is thereby influenced by it, his desires coming from without, but rather that his unconscious spits the world out and at the same time desires it. His craving for Lolita has been produced by himself. He knows of himself, if not always consciously; his conception of himself is, however, another than the one that corresponds to the truth. He knows besides that his conception of Dolores, that is to say Lolita, does not correspond to the truth about her. He therefore lets the untruths converge. Should the one prove deficient, the other is ready to hand. As Humbert Humbert writes about himself under the pseudonym "Humbert Humbert", it becomes clear that his conception of the beloved evades not merely one truth, but two. By this means he is able to withstand the immanent contradiction. Inasmuch as the one untruth (Lolita) seeks its corrective in the other untruth (Humbert Humbert), the whole here shows itself to be a closed circle. Neither of the two need reach out into the external world to fetch anything for itself; rather, they are created by the narrator for the very purpose of completing one another. Within the context of the book this is entertaining enough, but if one considers the import of it in one's own life, it becomes clear that this work presents itself as a kind of parody. Whether intended or not, that painful fact cannot be escaped. The desideratum of Humbert's love is the untrue, and so too is its origin. What is painful about this is, to my impression, the reflection carried out at this point in the book, namely that one's own desire consists in a deception produced by oneself. One might well suggest here that a society favouring and privileging the exchange of commodities contributes to this confusion. But I have neither the inclination nor the space for it in this text.

The passage immediately following this quotation also shows the narrator's fear in the face of the passing of time. He (Nabokov) writes:

"She will be thirteen on 1 January. In two years she will cease to be a nymphet. […] *That* Lolita, *My* Lolita, poor Catullus would lose for ever."[^4]

The narrator shows here that he knows full well that the object of his craving will disappear. He knows besides — for he has already admitted as much — that *his Lolita* does not exist in reality. He is playing, then, with the unreal conception of an unreal object. For we see over the course of the book how Dolores cannot endure the torment. How she weeps every night. How, after a treatment in hospital, she does not let the chance slip and escapes the narrator. Although everything is tolerable inside the narrator's head, the whole thing collapses in reality. But does it founder here upon contradictions immanent to love? Caution is called for, since what is at issue here is, to my mind, not a love but rather a desire, a degenerate obsession on the part of the narrator, one not conceived as reciprocal from the side of its object. The gravest criticisms levelled at the book are directed precisely at the fact that Dolores is given no voice of her own, that she is treated like a stowaway. This, however, is what is essential to the story itself. Nabokov attempts, that is, through the narrator's hand, to persuade the reader subliminally that what is at issue here is a love. At the same time he withholds from Dolores every possibility of reflecting the desire back. The voice that Dolores does not receive constitutes the foundation of the crime, for had she been granted a voice of her own, one would run the risk that the story might after all present itself as a degenerate kind of love. The narrator quotes Lolita contradicting him now and then; he never, however, lets it get out of hand. Dolores must remain voiceless if the depravity of the desire is not to be waved away.

Nabokov thereby describes a process that every love, or rather every desire, is capable of undergoing. The unremitting reification of Dolores, the reified Dolores who is called Lolita, is — leaving aside the other discomfiting facts — a symptom of a failed love that appears all too frequently in reality. The digitalisation of the dating sphere has the consequence that potential partners are left, for longer stretches, without protection against their own idealisation. Although this was always the case — before the age of dating apps one could idealise a person just as well — the process during which one is made to figure as a mere ideal is prolonged. For the process of any given dating app rests upon fantasy. One can, to be sure, converse with a potential match, but the first impression here does not consist in the conversation, but in the photographs, in the banal facts about that person's interests. Idealisation therefore predominates throughout the process of getting to know someone, whereby the possibility arises of no longer being able to strike it out. Reification thus becomes the protagonist of modern love more often than the person herself. Reification has, in accordance with its nature, no voice of its own, but only the one lent to it. A further case of this is the idealisation of celebrities. Take, for example, Taylor Swift, who has built up a parasocial relationship with a fanbase of millions of people. Her presence on stage may well be breathtaking; my aim here is not to dispute that. But the fans who love her so are not connected to the person; they are attached to the conception of "Taylor Swift". The Taylor Swift discussed in newspaper articles and on the evening news is not Taylor Swift, but a hypostatised reification that allows people to lend her their own voices. The celebrity "Taylor Swift" is able, now and then, to make a statement in view of some recent event, yet through her own socialisation every statement remains veiled by the hidden motives her fans imagine for themselves.

In both cases here we are dealing with a certain absence, as with Humbert. For in both cases the bodily aspects of the persons are present, which is to say available, but the voice is always a distorted one, or rather a non-existent one.

In the first case it is the absence of the potential match. An absence, however, that is covered over by the dating apps' staging the constant presence of their users. According to the dating apps, they have made the process of dating easier by abolishing its temporal limits. It is henceforth possible to find a partner at any hour. One need no longer wait for the end of the working day, or for the weekend; every moment is now the right one to set out in search of love. The structure of the violence that Humbert does to Dolores, by transforming her into Lolita and robbing her of her voice, is to be observed in the process of the dating app as well. This time subliminally incited by the company responsible and carried out by the users themselves. What presents itself, then, as the presence of an incidental match is in reality that match's absence. It seems to me important to remark at this point that the violence meant here is not the rape of a child, but the conceptual structure of violence that first makes such a rape possible. The structure of the violence consists in robbing the other, the reified human being, of every voice save the one lent to them by the signifier, so that such outrages can be normalised. I do not wish to discuss this here at any length, but the rise of the male "supremacy propaganda" disseminated on social media, and the accompanying perceptions of other people, above all in this case women, certainly contribute to the rise in recorded offences of recent years. Through the rise in violence, however, there also shows itself the hopeful readiness no longer to let it be swept under the carpet.[^5] One can only hope that the cause for this increase in the numbers stems purely from the readiness to stand up and defy the perpatrators. I don´t intend to amuse such idealisations however.

In the second case the absence is somewhat more complicated, since Swift is aware of her own dissimulation. Unlike in the first case, where the reification proceeds unconsciously from both sides and is staged by a third party, here it proceeds consciously from at least one side. For Swift deploys this invented false identity in order to maintain her status in the world of contemporary culture. Through calculated marketing devices this false identity is disseminated and staged as the *real* one. The Taylor Swift of the masses is the Taylor Swift of the real Taylor Swift's fantasy, which is to say the reified Taylor Swift. For she has had to reify herself in order to be capable of extending her success. This reification thus takes up the place that was, so to speak, reserved for it, but here it is not laid to rest. In order to maintain the status, the reification must change, mutate, keep pace with the times. The reification must therefore make itself capable of being hypostatised. The reified Taylor Swift is, in view of the real Taylor Swift's conduct, hypostatised by fans. Suddenly the sham Swift exists alongside the real one without this creating any difficulty. One has thereby been able to expose the lie that the marketing promise does not concern the real celebrity, and at the same time to maintain the sham celebrity.[6](#user-content-fn-6) Just as Humbert Humbert represents the untrue identity of the narrator and Lolita the untrue identity of Dolores, so likewise the hypostatisation represents one untrue identity and the reification a second, equally untrue. Here the hypostatisation can seek its corrective in the reification and vice versa. This has the consequence that Swift's voice is divided in two. She has robbed herself of her voice so that it can be attributed to one of the untrue Swifts. In this way she remains, like Lolita, voiceless, in order that the obsession, or rather the desire, for her may be maintained. As in Lolita's case, both invented identities spring from the same hand. Here, however, Swift is not attempting to hold her own world together, but the world of her fans. Instead of deceiving herself in order to be able to go on believing that she is loved, she deceives the public in order to convince it that she loves it. Swift is, in her case, the opposite of what Dolores represents. She attempts to hold the gulf between the unreal and the real as far apart as possible, so that she may profit from it as much as possible. Whereas the rupture in Dolores's case — the weeping at night — represents the failure, the rupture in Swift's case is by contrast a sign of success. Her voicelessness is precisely what grants her power, whereas in the case of Dolores it testifies to her powerlessness.

I come, I believe, to the end of my reflections. A mere attempt is what one had better call it. The core themes of the book conceal themselves behind a curtain of disgust. It is worth reading the book, in my opinion, provided one does not succumb to this disgust. For one does recoil in disgust from the content, and the sad fact is that one must leave it aside in order to be able to go on reading the book. Whether this came about by design I do not know. Although the material is abrasive, it testifies nonetheless to Nabokov's insights.
